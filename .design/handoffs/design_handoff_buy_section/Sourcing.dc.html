Where Compare prices lands
The rail draws the final map: Sell 6, Buy 2, Catalogue 4, Money 4, Insight 4, Setup 3 — 23 rows and Today, from 33 and Today. Every cut is argued in Rail.dc.html, which is the authority if a frame disagrees.
Compare's table is the Quotes in hand block on this panel, and the difference is the thing that made Compare wrong: here the comparison is for a stated quantity — 20 Box, the amount you would actually buy — so the prices are the ones you would pay. Three quotes, best marked, the others as the percentage more they want. The row also notices that one asker wanted 40 Box, which is a different price again.
What the screen is for
Demand you could not serve. One line per item however it was spelled — findSourcingLeadByText already folds "malper hinges" onto the same lead — so the count is people, not typing.
The judgement it makes
Three or more different askers is demand; once or twice is a job. Both groups are on screen, because the shop may know something about the one customer that the count does not.
The loop it closes
Settling a lead means stocking it or answering "we do not carry it" — and the stocked ones carry what they sold since, so the queue can be judged by what it earned rather than by how long it is.
Three readings the data already held: distinct askers rather than asks, so one persistent customer cannot look like demand — and Okello asking twice is shown as exactly that. The margin the lead would earn at your own pricing rule, before anything is bought: 18,500 at 30% is a 24,100 shelf price. And who the askers are — four of the five buy iron sheets here, so the hinge sells beside something they already come for, which is a better argument for stocking it than the ask count alone.
